What to look for on your first visit to a kids martial arts class

Most academies offer a free trial class or let you watch one before signing up. Twenty minutes on the sideline tells you more than any conversation in the office. Here is what to watch for.

On this page

  1. Mat supervision
  2. Bathroom and changing room policy
  3. How instructors correct kids
  4. Reading the room
  5. What to do if something feels off

Mat supervision

  • More than one adult is present for a kids class of any size, not one coach alone with a room full of children.
  • Coaches can see the whole mat, not just the group they're working with directly.
  • Parents can watch from the lobby or a viewing area without needing to ask permission.

Bathroom and changing room policy

Ask what the policy is rather than guess from what you see in twenty minutes. A good answer separates adults and children, or sets clear times, and names who is responsible for checking. If a child needs help with a uniform or a belt, the answer should involve a parent or another child's presence, not a coach alone with a child behind a closed door.

How instructors correct kids

Watch how a coach corrects a child who is struggling or misbehaving. Correction should be verbal, direct, and proportionate, in front of the class, not physical beyond what is needed to demonstrate technique, and not humiliating. A coach who singles a child out for one on one attention away from the group, repeatedly, is worth a question about why.

Reading the room

Do the kids look like they want to be there. Do other parents seem comfortable stepping in and out of the space. Is there a front desk or someone paying attention to who comes and goes, or does anyone wander in off the street. None of this replaces asking direct questions; see the full eight questions to ask and what a certified academy has already put in writing before you ever visit.

What to do if something feels off

A first visit rarely produces certainty. It produces a feeling, and the useful thing is to turn that feeling into a question rather than sit on it. If supervision looked thin, ask how many adults are rostered for that class. If a coach seemed over familiar with one child, ask what the policy is on one on one contact. An academy that runs things properly will answer plainly and will not treat the question as an accusation.

Take the answer at face value the first time, then watch whether practice matches it on your second visit. Policies that exist only as answers are the ones that fail. If the answer is vague, or the question is brushed off, that is information too, and you are allowed to walk away without explaining yourself.
