SafeSport training for jiu jitsu coaches

Most jiu jitsu coaches have never taken SafeSport training, and most have never been asked to. Here is what it is, who it is normally required of, and why Safe Academy 360 requires it of every coach it certifies.

What it is

SafeSport training is abuse prevention education delivered by the U.S. Center for SafeSport, the body that handles abuse and misconduct across the U.S. Olympic and Paralympic movement. The training covers recognizing grooming and boundary violations, the forms abuse and misconduct take in sport, and what a coach is expected to do when they see or hear something.

Safe Academy 360 is not affiliated with or endorsed by the Center. SafeSport is the Center's trademark. Coaches complete the training with the Center directly, and the program's part is to require it and keep the certificate on file. See the trademark notice.

Who is required to take it

Inside the Olympic and Paralympic movement, coaches and many other adults are required to complete it. Judo and wrestling coaches at clubs under their national governing bodies will recognize it for that reason.

Most jiu jitsu academies sit outside that structure. No governing body requires SafeSport training of them, no law does, and no affiliation does as a condition of membership. The result is that the most widely recognized abuse prevention course in American sport is unfamiliar at the academies where adults and children share a mat most closely.

Why it matters at a jiu jitsu academy

Jiu jitsu is taught through contact. Coaches adjust positions with their hands, kids and adults train in the same room, and a coach's relationship with a young student can last a decade. That is not a problem. It is a setting where the boundaries need to be named, because nothing about the sport names them by default.

The training gives a coach language for that. What grooming looks like from the outside. Why a private message to a twelve year old is a policy question and not a judgment call. What to do with a disclosure that comes halfway through a class. A coach who has never been taught these things is relying on instinct, and instinct is what abusers count on.

How a coach completes it

The coach goes to the Center's own site, creates an account, and takes the course online. The Center sets the cost, the length, and the renewal cycle, and publishes them there. Safe Academy 360 does not deliver the course, does not charge for it, and does not change what it costs. The $5 per month per coach covers the review and tracking of the certificate, not the course.

When the course is complete, the coach downloads or saves the completion certificate. That is the document the program needs.

What goes on file

The coach uploads the certificate through the platform, from a phone or a computer. A reviewer reads it as a document, records it against the coach with its own expiry date, and the coach's credential shows it as current until that date. When it approaches, the platform shows the date ahead of time so renewal is scheduled rather than discovered.

SafeSport training is one of the five items every certified coach holds, alongside a public records screen, mandated reporter training, CPR and first aid, and the academy's signed code of conduct.

What it does not do

Completing the course does not make a coach safe, and Safe Academy 360 does not say it does. It means the coach has been taught what to look for and what to do. What they do with that is what a written code of conduct and an owner who enforces it are for.

An uploaded certificate is reviewed as a document and kept on file. It is not verified with the Center. The program says "on file," not "verified," about it for that reason.
