How to ask a gym about screening without accusing anyone

Most parents who want to ask about coach screening never do, because the question feels like an accusation. It isn't one. Here is how to ask it so it sounds like what it is: a normal question any parent should be able to ask.

Why this question feels hard to ask

Asking "do you screen your coaches" can sound like you suspect this specific academy of something, when really you would ask the same question anywhere. The fix is not avoiding the question. It's asking it as a standard practice, the same way you'd ask about class sizes or belt requirements.

Three ways to ask it

  • "What do you check before someone joins or starts coaching here?" A general, practice-focused version that works for coaches and other members alike.
  • "I ask every academy this, so don't take it personally: how do you screen coaches, and how do you check who joins?" Naming that it's routine takes the edge off for both of you.
  • "Is there a written safety standard I could see?" Lets the academy show you rather than explain, which is a better answer if one exists.

What to listen for in the answer

A good answer describes what is actually checked and is honest that no check catches everyone. Should give you pause: "we trust our coaches," a guarantee that everyone is safe, or a reaction that treats the question as offensive rather than reasonable. If you'd rather not ask at all

Some parents would rather check independently than ask in person. A certified academy displays a placard with a certificate number you can look up on verify without asking anyone anything. See the directory for academies that have completed the program.

What a weak answer sounds like

Most answers fall into three shapes. The first is specific: who is screened, what is searched, how often it is repeated, and where it is recorded. That is a good answer whether or not the academy uses any particular program.

The second is reassuring but empty. Everyone here is vetted. We have known our coaches for years. Neither describes a process, and length of acquaintance is not a check. The third is defensive, where the question itself is treated as an insult. That reaction tells you how a concern raised later would be received, which is arguably more useful than the original answer.

None of this requires you to be an expert. You are listening for whether a process exists and whether someone can describe it without improvising.
